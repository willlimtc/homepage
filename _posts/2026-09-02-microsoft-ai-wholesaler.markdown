Hello and happy fall, everyone!

Last Sunday I did what half of Southern California does on a Sunday: I went to Costco. I walked in for paper towels. I walked out with paper towels, a rotisserie chicken, a three-pack of olive oil I did not need, new windshield wipers, and a hot dog.

And somewhere in that checkout line — surrounded by a cart I did not plan — it hit me.

*This is Microsoft's AI strategy.*

I have spent this year sitting with enterprise customers trying to figure out what to do about AI, and I could not unsee it. So here is my read on where Microsoft is heading — through the lens of the warehouse club.

> **A quick disclaimer.** I work at Microsoft, but nothing here is an official position or a roadmap. This is one person's read on publicly available information — a guess, written because thinking in public is how I learn. Not investment advice, and please don't make a product decision because a guy on the internet made a Costco joke. 😄

## What Costco Actually Sells

Costco is not really in the groceries business.

Costco sells **curated decisions**. A normal big-box store gives you dozens of options and leaves you to sort it out. Costco gives you one or two, with an implicit promise: somebody already did the homework. Somebody tasted the olive oil, checked the supplier, argued about the price.

You are not paying for the product. You are paying to *not have to think about the product*.

Then there is the less romantic half: **Costco relentlessly builds warehouses.** Curation means nothing if you cannot physically serve the member.

That is the part people underrate about Microsoft right now. The enormous, headline-generating investment in datacenter capacity? That is the warehouse buildout. Compute where the workloads are, regions where the data is legally required to stay. You cannot be anyone's wholesaler without shelf space — and in AI, shelf space is measured in megawatts.

## The AI Aisle Is Overwhelming

Here is what I see in my day job: customers who are *paralyzed*. Not because they doubt AI matters — that argument ended a while ago. They are stuck because the shelf is absurd.

There are north of eleven thousand models in the catalog, and a new one lands seemingly every week with a benchmark chart proving it is the best. Six months ago the obvious choice was one model. Today it is a different one.

So the question I get is almost never "should we do AI." It is:

> "How do we pick, and how badly are we going to regret it?"

That is not a technology problem. That is a **shopping** problem — exactly the problem warehouse clubs were invented to solve.

## It's Not One Store. It's Many Departments Under One Roof.

Nobody joins Costco for a single aisle. You came for groceries, but there is also the pharmacy. The optical counter. The tire center. The food court. Same membership, same roof, wildly different needs met. You went in for tires and left with a rotisserie chicken — that is not an accident, that is the design.

Microsoft's AI story reads the same way. There is no single "AI product" you go shopping for. There is AI already vetted and built into the department you were coming to anyway:

- **Modern work** — AI inside the tools your people already open every morning
- **Business applications** — AI inside the processes that actually run the company
- **Cloud and infrastructure** — AI where your workloads and your data already live
- **Security** — AI defending the whole estate

You do not shop for AI. You shop for the department, and the AI is already on the shelf.

There is even a house brand. Microsoft is increasingly building and shipping [its own models](https://microsoft.ai/) alongside everyone else's. I would not oversell this — nobody is calling them Kirkland Signature yet, and that kind of reputation takes years to earn. But the strategic logic is the one every serious retailer eventually lands on: a private label is leverage. It means you can always stock the shelf, and no single supplier can hold the store hostage.

**And then there is the part that makes the whole thing work.**

Here is what people miss about Costco: you trust the pharmacy for exactly the same reason you trust the olive oil. **The buying standard does not change by department.** Costco is willing to put its name on your prescriptions and your brake safety, not just your snacks. That uniform standard *is* the actual product.

Security is Microsoft's version of that standard. It is not a department sitting beside the others — it is the floor underneath all of them. Identity, compliance, data governance, threat protection, applied the same way whether the AI is drafting an email, touching a customer record, or running a workload in your cloud.

That matters more than ever, because AI invented new ways for things to go sideways. Data walking out the door. Prompt injection. Compliance exposure nobody modeled for. In that world, the thing worth paying for is not the cleverest model — it is knowing somebody with decades of security scar tissue already stood behind everything on the shelf.

> Costco's return policy removes the risk of **buying**.
> Microsoft's security posture removes the risk of **deploying**.

Same promise, different aisle: **buy with confidence.**

## So Here's the Whole Metaphor

> **Copilot** is your membership card.
> **The solution areas** are the departments.
> **First-party models** are the house brand.
> **The datacenters** are the warehouses.
> **Security** is the buying standard that never changes, no matter which aisle you are in.

## Where the Analogy Breaks

I would not be writing a useful post if I just kept polishing my own metaphor until it shined. So let me break it on purpose.

**The card is not required for everything.** Copilot is genuinely a membership — per person, per month, renewed. But plenty of AI at Microsoft is nothing like that. Foundry and Azure AI services are pure consumption: pay for what you use, no card, no annual fee. Microsoft runs a members-only club *and* an open wholesale counter side by side. Costco would never.

**Costco has to earn you back every year.** This one is uncomfortable and worth saying plainly. A Costco membership has essentially zero switching cost — cancel it, walk away, shop across the street. That loyalty is *earned*. Enterprise platform loyalty is a different animal, and some of it is structural rather than earned. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

**And then the one that really breaks it: Costco does not know you.**

Your membership is anonymous. The warehouse has no idea what you are working on, who you are working with, what you promised a customer on Friday, or what is buried in a thread from three weeks ago. It is a fantastic store — but it is a store you walk out of.

A platform is something you build *into*. And this is where the real differentiation lives — not in the models, not in the price, not even in the shelf. It is in [WorkIQ](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/microsoft365copilotblog/a-closer-look-at-work-iq/4499789): the intelligence already inside your own organization. Your work patterns, your collaborators, your documents, your history.

A competitor can match a model. A competitor can match a price. A competitor can even match the shelf.

**Nobody can hand you your own institutional memory.**

## Nobody Can Predict This. That's the Point.

AI is moving faster than any technology I have worked with, and let me be honest: nobody can precisely predict where this lands. Not the labs, not the analysts, and definitely not me. Model leadership has already changed hands repeatedly and it will change hands again — probably before you finish your next quarterly plan.

Which is exactly why the business model matters more than the model.

If nobody can win the "best model" race permanently, the thing worth building is everything *around* it: the capacity to serve you, a curated shelf so you are not guessing, a private label so no supplier can squeeze you, one uniform standard of security underneath all of it, and a context layer that makes you smarter the longer you stay.

That is not a bet on which model wins. That is a bet on making **you** successful regardless of which one does.

So my advice, for whatever a guy with a cart full of olive oil is worth: stop agonizing over which model to standardize on — that decision has a shorter shelf life than the rotisserie chicken. **Buy the membership, not the SKU.**
